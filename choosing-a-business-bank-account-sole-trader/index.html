Choosing a business bank account as a sole trader

If you search for the "best business bank account for a sole trader", you will find many lists that rank providers. No single account suits everyone, though. The right choice depends on how you work, how you handle money, and how you plan to keep your tax records. This guide covers whether you need a separate account, what to compare, and how your choice fits with Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax.

Do sole traders legally need a business bank account?

No. According to business.gov.uk, sole traders and ordinary partnerships are not legally required to have a business bank account. You can run your business through a personal account if you choose.

The same guidance also explains why many people choose to separate their finances anyway. Keeping business money apart from personal money makes accounting simpler:

  • Business transactions are easier to find. You do not have to pick out client payments and supplier costs from your groceries and household bills.
  • Cash flow is easier to track. You can see what has come in and gone out of the business without any personal spending mixed in.
  • Records for tax are simpler. When the account only contains business activity, preparing your figures for HMRC takes less sorting.

You should also check the terms of your personal account. Some personal accounts may not allow business use. Read your own bank's terms or ask the bank directly.

Why this matters more under MTD for Income Tax

MTD for Income Tax is being introduced in stages based on your qualifying income:

  • From 6 April 2026: required if your qualifying income was more than £50,000 in the 2024 to 2025 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2027: required if your qualifying income is more than £30,000 in the 2025 to 2026 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2028: required if your qualifying income is more than £20,000 in the 2026 to 2027 tax year.

Qualifying income is your total income from self-employment and property before expenses (your turnover). It does not include employment (PAYE) income, a partner's share of partnership profit, dividends, the State Pension or private pensions. For jointly owned property, only your share counts.

GOV.UK gives this example. Someone with £25,000 of rental income and £27,000 of self-employment income has £52,000 of qualifying income. A jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000.

If you are within MTD for Income Tax, you must:

  • keep digital records
  • use compatible software to send quarterly updates of your income and expenses to HMRC
  • sign up before your start date

Quarterly updates are summaries, not tax returns. Under standard periods, the quarters end on 5 July, 5 October, 5 January and 5 April. The deadlines are 7 August, 7 November, 7 February and 7 May. If you choose calendar periods (ending 30 June, 30 September, 31 December and 31 March), the deadlines are the same.

This is where a separate account helps. If you send figures four times a year, you need your business income and expenses to be easy to identify each quarter. A dedicated account will not keep your records for you, but it removes a lot of the sorting.

What to compare when choosing an account

business.gov.uk describes two broad types of provider.

Traditional banks can take longer to set up. They may offer branches, overdrafts and loans.

App-based providers can be set up online quickly. They may offer limited overdrafts.

Beyond that, these are the practical questions to ask any provider. You will need to check each provider's current terms yourself, because fees and features change.

1. Fees and charges

Look at the monthly fee, if there is one, and the charges for the things you actually do. These might include card payments, bank transfers, cash deposits and international payments. An account that looks cheap can cost more if it charges for the transactions you make most often.

2. How you get paid

Consider how your customers or tenants pay you.

  • If you take a lot of cash, find out how and where you can deposit it, and what that costs.
  • If you mainly receive bank transfers, check how easily you can match incoming payments to invoices or rent due.

3. Access to credit

If you might need an overdraft or loan, ask what the provider offers and on what terms. business.gov.uk notes that app-based providers may offer limited overdrafts, so this could be a deciding factor.

4. Support

Think about how you would want to get help if something went wrong. Some people prefer a branch. Others are happy with in-app chat or phone support.

5. Whether it works with your record-keeping software

This is especially important for MTD. GOV.UK's guidance on software says to:

  • use its software finder
  • choose between software that creates digital records and bridging software that works with spreadsheets
  • check that the software supports your income sources and accounting period

Free options exist for simple tax affairs.

When comparing bank accounts, check whether your chosen software can import transactions from the account, or whether you would need to export and upload them yourself. Confirm this with both the software provider and the bank rather than assuming it will work.

A simple setup for landlords

Landlords often receive rent and pay for repairs, agent fees and other costs. If you also have self-employment income, both count towards qualifying income. Your software must support both income sources.

Suppose you let one property and also do freelance work. You might keep both through a single business account and label each transaction by income source in your software. Or you might prefer two accounts to keep the property and the trading activity fully apart. Neither approach is required. Pick the one that makes your quarterly figures easiest to produce.

For jointly owned property, remember that only your share counts towards qualifying income. If you and a co-owner receive rent into a shared account, agree how you will each record your share.

Working with an accountant or bookkeeper

Under MTD for Income Tax, you can have one main agent at a time and any number of supporting agents.

  • A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax.
  • A supporting agent has limited access and can only see business and property income details.

An agent, such as an accountant or bookkeeper, can sign you up with your permission.

If you use an agent, ask them which bank and software combinations they work with most easily. A clean business account can make their job quicker.

Where an app-based account may fit

If you want an account you can open online quickly, and you do not need a branch or a large overdraft, an app-based provider is one option to consider. Tide is one such business current account. Before you apply, check its current fees, eligibility and features against the questions above, as you would with any provider.

If you are not sure about timing

You do not have to wait for MTD to apply to you before opening a business account. Separating your finances now gives you clean records to work from when your start date arrives.

Two more points from GOV.UK may help:

  • Mistakes can be fixed in later quarters. Quarterly updates are cumulative: each covers from the start of the tax year to the end of that period. You can correct earlier records without resubmitting earlier quarters.
  • No penalty points for late updates in 2026 to 2027. HMRC will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates for the 2026 to 2027 tax year. You must still sign up and meet your obligations. For other penalty rules, check GOV.UK.

Checklist

  1. Work out your qualifying income using the GOV.UK definition.
  2. Check which MTD start date, if any, applies to you.
  3. Decide whether a separate business account would simplify your records. It is not legally required.
  4. Compare fees, how you get paid, credit, support and software compatibility.
  5. Use the GOV.UK software finder to choose compatible software.
  6. Talk to your accountant or bookkeeper if you use one.
  7. Sign up for MTD for Income Tax before your start date if it applies to you.
