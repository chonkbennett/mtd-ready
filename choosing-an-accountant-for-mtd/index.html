Choosing an accountant for Making Tax Digital: what to ask

Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax changes how sole traders and landlords keep records and report to HMRC. You do not have to use an accountant. If you do, choose one who understands the new requirements and can explain how they will handle them for you. This guide sets out what MTD involves, what an accountant can do under the new rules, and the questions to ask before you sign up with anyone.

First, check whether and when MTD applies to you

Before you speak to an accountant, find out roughly where you stand. According to GOV.UK, MTD for Income Tax is required:

  • from 6 April 2026 for sole traders and landlords whose qualifying income was more than £50,000 in the 2024 to 2025 tax year
  • from 6 April 2027 if qualifying income is more than £30,000 in the 2025 to 2026 tax year
  • from 6 April 2028 if qualifying income is more than £20,000 in the 2026 to 2027 tax year

Qualifying income is your total income from self-employment and property before expenses, which means your turnover. The following do not count:

  • employment (PAYE) income
  • a partner's share of partnership profit
  • dividends
  • the State Pension and private pensions

For jointly owned property, only your share counts.

GOV.UK gives two examples:

  • Someone with £25,000 of rental income and £27,000 of self-employment income has £52,000 of qualifying income.
  • A jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000 of qualifying income.

These figures help you start the conversation. A good accountant should be able to confirm your qualifying income and your start date. They should also explain how they reached that answer.

What an accountant can do under MTD

Under MTD for Income Tax, everyone in scope must:

  • keep digital records
  • use compatible software to send quarterly updates of income and expenses to HMRC
  • sign up before their start date

An agent, such as an accountant or bookkeeper, can sign you up with your permission.

GOV.UK also sets out two types of agent:

  • Main agent. You can have one main agent at a time. A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax.
  • Supporting agent. You can have any number of supporting agents. A supporting agent has limited access and can only see your business and property income details.

This matters when you choose. For example, you might use one firm as your main agent and a bookkeeper as a supporting agent. Make sure everyone knows their role.

Questions to ask a prospective accountant

1. Will you act as my main agent or a supporting agent?

Ask which role they expect to take. If you already work with a bookkeeper or another adviser, agree who will be the main agent. Only one person can hold that role at a time.

2. Will you sign me up for MTD, and when?

You need to be signed up before your start date. Ask whether signing up is part of their service and how they will get your permission. Also ask what information they will need from you.

3. Which software do you use, and does it suit my income?

GOV.UK advises:

  • using its software finder
  • choosing between software that creates digital records and bridging software that works with spreadsheets
  • checking that the software supports your income sources and accounting period

Ask the accountant:

  • Which software they work with, and why it suits you.
  • Whether it handles all your income sources, such as self-employment and property, or several properties.
  • Whether it supports your accounting period.
  • Whether bridging software is an option if you prefer to keep using spreadsheets.

GOV.UK notes that free options exist for simple tax affairs. Ask whether one of those would be enough for you, or whether there is a clear reason to pay for software.

4. Who keeps the digital records: me or you?

This is often where misunderstandings begin. Some accountants expect you to record transactions yourself. Others take on the bookkeeping. Agree the following in writing:

  • who enters income and expenses
  • how often they are entered
  • how receipts and invoices are stored

5. How will you handle quarterly updates and deadlines?

GOV.UK sets out the quarterly periods and deadlines:

Standard period ends Calendar period ends Deadline
5 July 30 June 7 August
5 October 30 September 7 November
5 January 31 December 7 February
5 April 31 March 7 May

Standard periods start on 6 April. Calendar periods start on 1 April. Both have the same deadlines.

Ask these questions:

  • Which type of period do they recommend for you, and why?
  • When do they need your records each quarter?
  • Who submits the update?
  • How will they confirm it has been sent?

6. How do you deal with corrections?

Quarterly updates are cumulative. Each one covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of that quarter. This means earlier records can be corrected without resubmitting earlier quarters.

Ask how the accountant spots and fixes errors. Ask, too, whether they will tell you when they make a correction.

7. What happens at the end of the year?

GOV.UK states that quarterly updates are summaries, not tax returns. Ask the accountant to explain what is needed at the end of the tax year, and whether their fee covers it. For the official year-end requirements, check GOV.UK.

8. What is included in the fee, and what costs extra?

No fee figures are given here because prices vary. Ask for a written list of what is included, such as:

  • sign-up
  • software
  • bookkeeping
  • quarterly updates
  • year-end work
  • property income
  • HMRC queries

Also ask what triggers extra charges.

9. Can you handle both property and self-employment income?

If you have both, or you own property jointly, make sure they are comfortable with your mix of income. They should know that only your share of jointly owned property counts towards qualifying income.

10. What do you advise on separating business finances?

Sole traders are not legally required to have a business bank account. However, GOV.UK's business guidance says separating personal and business finances makes accounting simpler:

  • business transactions are easier to find
  • cash flow is easier to track
  • records for tax are simpler

The guidance also notes a trade-off between account types:

  • Traditional banks can take longer to set up but may offer branches, overdrafts and loans.
  • App-based providers set up online quickly but may offer limited overdrafts.

A helpful accountant will discuss which option suits the way you work, rather than simply recommending one product.

Penalties in the first year

HMRC will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates for the 2026 to 2027 tax year. Some accountants may present MTD as an emergency. They should instead explain this point accurately and help you build good habits early. For penalty rules beyond that year, check GOV.UK.

Red flags to watch for

  • Vague answers about who keeps records or who submits updates.
  • Unclear statements about whether they will be your main or supporting agent.
  • Claims that one particular software package is superior to all others without a reason linked to your income or accounting period.
  • Fees that are not explained in writing.
  • Promises of a particular tax outcome.

Quick checklist before you decide

  • Confirm your qualifying income and likely MTD start date.
  • Agree whether the accountant will be your main or supporting agent.
  • Agree who signs you up, and when.
  • Choose compatible software that supports your income sources and accounting period.
  • Settle who keeps the digital records and how often.
  • Choose standard or calendar quarterly periods and note the deadlines.
  • Get the fee and what it includes in writing.
  • Decide whether to separate your business finances.

A good accountant will answer these points clearly and put the arrangements in writing.
