Qualifying income for MTD: why turnover counts, not profit

The short answer

Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax uses turnover, not profit, to decide who must join. GOV.UK defines qualifying income as your total income from self-employment and property before expenses.

This catches out many sole traders and landlords. You might have high costs, low profit and still be over the threshold. This guide explains what counts, what does not, how the thresholds work, and what to do if you are in scope.

What counts as qualifying income

According to GOV.UK, qualifying income is:

  • Total income from self-employment, before any expenses are taken off
  • Total income from property, before any expenses are taken off

You add these together to get one figure. GOV.UK gives this example:

£25,000 of rental income plus £27,000 of self-employment income is £52,000 of qualifying income.

Neither income source is over £50,000 on its own, but together they are. The combined turnover is what matters.

What does not count

GOV.UK lists several types of income that are not part of qualifying income:

  • Employment (PAYE) income
  • A partner's share of partnership profit
  • Dividends
  • The State Pension
  • Private pensions

If you have a salaried job and a small side business, only the side business turnover counts. Your salary does not. The same applies to pension income and dividends from a limited company.

If you have other kinds of income not on this list and you are unsure whether they count, check the qualifying income guidance on GOV.UK. Do not assume either way.

Jointly owned property

If you own a property with someone else, only your share of the income counts towards your qualifying income. GOV.UK's example:

A jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000.

Each owner then adds their £25,000 share to any other self-employment or property turnover they have. The total for each person is then compared against the threshold.

Why turnover and not profit

The rule is defined as income before expenses, so you need to look at your top-line figure. Your profit figure is not the right one to use. This matters most for businesses with high costs, such as trades that buy in materials. It also matters for landlords with large repair or letting costs.

Suppose a sole trader brings in £60,000 a year but spends £45,000 on materials, fuel and equipment. Their profit is £15,000. It would be easy to think they are well below any MTD threshold. Their qualifying income is £60,000, though, because the expenses are not deducted. On the facts in this example, their turnover is over £50,000. So if those were their figures for 2024 to 2025, they would be within MTD from 6 April 2026.

Suppose instead a landlord collects £35,000 in rent with no other self-employment or property income. Their costs bring their profit down to £10,000. Their qualifying income is still £35,000. That is not over £50,000, so the 6 April 2026 start would not apply. But if £35,000 were their figure for 2025 to 2026, it would be over £30,000, and MTD would apply from 6 April 2027.

The practical lesson is simple. When you check whether MTD applies to you, use turnover from your records. Do not use the profit figure from your tax return.

The thresholds and start dates

GOV.UK sets out a phased introduction. Each start date is based on your qualifying income in an earlier tax year:

MTD required from If qualifying income was more than In the tax year
6 April 2026 £50,000 2024 to 2025
6 April 2027 £30,000 2025 to 2026
6 April 2028 £20,000 2026 to 2027

Note the wording: more than the threshold. For each date, the test looks back to a specific tax year's turnover.

Some questions are not covered here. These include what happens if your income later falls, and whether any exemptions apply to you. For these, check the eligibility guidance on GOV.UK.

Working out your own figure

A simple way to approach it:

  1. List your self-employment turnover for the relevant tax year. Use the total before expenses.
  2. List your property income for the same year, before expenses. For jointly owned property, include only your share.
  3. Leave out PAYE income, partnership profit shares, dividends, the State Pension and private pensions.
  4. Add the figures together. This is your qualifying income.
  5. Compare it with the threshold for the matching tax year in the table above.

You may be unsure how a particular receipt should be treated. Examples include VAT, overseas property or one-off income. GOV.UK's "Work out your qualifying income" guidance is the place to confirm this.

If you are in scope: what MTD asks of you

GOV.UK says people within MTD for Income Tax must:

  • Keep digital records of their income and expenses
  • Use compatible software to send quarterly updates to HMRC
  • Sign up before their start date

Quarterly updates

If you use standard periods, your quarters and deadlines are:

Period Deadline
6 April to 5 July 7 August
6 July to 5 October 7 November
6 October to 5 January 7 February
6 January to 5 April 7 May

You can choose calendar periods instead: 1 April to 30 June, 30 September, 31 December and 31 March. The deadlines are the same.

Two points from GOV.UK make the process less daunting:

  • Updates are cumulative. Each update covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of that quarter. If you spot a mistake in an earlier quarter, you can correct it in the next update. You do not need to resubmit the earlier quarter.
  • Updates are summaries, not tax returns.

GOV.UK also says HMRC will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates in the 2026 to 2027 tax year. This does not mean deadlines can be ignored. It is better to treat that first year as a chance to set up good habits.

Choosing software

GOV.UK recommends using its software finder. It suggests choosing between two main types:

  • Software that creates digital records, where you record transactions directly in the software
  • Bridging software, which works with spreadsheets you already keep

Whichever you choose, GOV.UK says to check that the software supports your income sources and your accounting period. For example, it needs to handle both self-employment and property if you have both. Free options exist for people with simple tax affairs, so it is worth comparing before you pay for anything.

Getting help from an agent

You do not have to manage MTD alone. GOV.UK says that sole traders and landlords can have:

  • One main agent at a time. They can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax.
  • Any number of supporting agents. They have limited access and can only see business and property income details.

An accountant or bookkeeper can also sign you up for MTD with your permission.

Keep business and personal money apart

Sole traders are not legally required to have a business bank account. GOV.UK's business guidance notes that keeping business and personal finances separate makes accounting simpler. Business transactions are easier to find, cash flow is easier to track and tax records are simpler. This also makes it easier to see your turnover clearly when you check your qualifying income.

When choosing an account, there are trade-offs. Traditional banks can take longer to set up but may offer branches, overdrafts and loans. App-based providers can be set up online quickly but may offer limited overdrafts.

If you keep separate records, software that connects to your business account can help you prepare quarterly updates. Check that it meets the GOV.UK compatibility points above.

Summary

  • MTD for Income Tax uses qualifying income. This is self-employment and property turnover before expenses, not profit.
  • PAYE income, partnership profit shares, dividends and pensions do not count. For joint property, count only your share.
  • The thresholds are more than £50,000 (from April 2026), £30,000 (from April 2027) and £20,000 (from April 2028). Each is based on an earlier tax year's figure.
  • If you are in scope, keep digital records, use compatible software for cumulative quarterly updates, and sign up before your start date.
  • For anything not covered here, check GOV.UK.
