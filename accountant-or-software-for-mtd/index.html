Accountant or software for MTD: which suits you?

Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax changes how many sole traders and landlords report to HMRC. If it applies to you, you have three main choices: handle it yourself with compatible software, hand it to an accountant, or combine the two. This guide helps you work out which suits your situation.

First, check whether and when MTD applies to you

Whether you need to act, and when, depends on your qualifying income. GOV.UK sets out a phased start:

  • From 6 April 2026: required if your qualifying income was more than £50,000 in the 2024 to 2025 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2027: required if your qualifying income is more than £30,000 in the 2025 to 2026 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2028: required if your qualifying income is more than £20,000 in the 2026 to 2027 tax year.

Qualifying income is your total income from self-employment and property before expenses (your turnover, not your profit). Several types of income do not count:

  • employment (PAYE) income
  • your share of partnership profit
  • dividends
  • the State Pension and private pensions

If you own property jointly, only your share counts.

GOV.UK gives two examples:

  • Someone with £25,000 of rental income and £27,000 of self-employment income has £52,000 of qualifying income. That is above the £50,000 threshold.
  • A jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000 of qualifying income.

The second example matters for landlords. Each owner's share can fall below a threshold that the property's total rent exceeds.

What MTD actually asks you to do

Before choosing between an accountant and software, it helps to know what the job involves. Under MTD for Income Tax you must:

  1. Keep digital records of your income and expenses.
  2. Use compatible software to send quarterly updates to HMRC.
  3. Sign up before your start date.

Quarterly updates follow standard periods ending 5 July, 5 October, 5 January and 5 April. The deadlines are 7 August, 7 November, 7 February and 7 May.

You can use calendar periods instead, ending 30 June, 30 September, 31 December and 31 March. These have the same deadlines. Check GOV.UK for how to choose calendar periods.

Two points make the quarterly work less daunting than it might sound:

  • Quarterly updates are summaries, not tax returns.
  • They are cumulative. Each update covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of that quarter. If you spot a mistake from an earlier quarter, you correct your records and the next update reflects it. You do not need to resubmit earlier quarters.

HMRC has also said it will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates in the 2026 to 2027 tax year. That gives some room while you settle into a routine. It is still sensible to aim for the deadlines from the start.

For anything beyond these points, such as end-of-year steps, other penalties or exemptions, check GOV.UK.

Option 1: Doing it yourself with software

Handling MTD yourself means choosing software, keeping your records in it (or linked to it), and sending the quarterly updates yourself.

GOV.UK's guidance on choosing software:

  • Use the GOV.UK software finder to find compatible products.
  • Choose between two types:
  • software that creates digital records for you
  • bridging software that works with spreadsheets you already keep
  • Check that the software supports your income sources (for example, self-employment, property, or both) and your accounting period.
  • Free options exist for people with simple tax affairs.

Doing it yourself may suit you if:

  • you have one straightforward income source, such as a single let property or a simple trade
  • you already keep tidy records, perhaps in a spreadsheet that bridging software could connect to
  • you are comfortable with technology and with checking figures
  • you want to keep direct control and see your numbers as you go

The trade-offs:

  • The time and responsibility are yours.
  • You will need to set up the software, keep records up to date and meet each deadline.
  • You will need to understand which income counts and how to categorise expenses.

If tax rules feel unfamiliar, the learning curve is real, even though quarterly updates are only summaries.

Option 2: Using an accountant

An accountant can take on most or all of the MTD work. GOV.UK sets out how agents work under MTD for Income Tax:

  • You can have one main agent at a time and any number of supporting agents.
  • A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax.
  • A supporting agent has limited access and can only see your business and property income details.
  • An agent, such as an accountant or bookkeeper, can sign you up for MTD with your permission.

An accountant may suit you if:

  • you have several income sources, such as a trade plus rental properties
  • you own property jointly and want help working out your share correctly
  • your records are disorganised or partly on paper, and you need help getting them into shape
  • you would rather spend your time on your business or properties than on admin
  • you want someone to check your figures before they reach HMRC

The trade-offs:

  • You pay for the service. Fees vary, so compare what different providers include.
  • You still need to supply accurate information on time. An accountant cannot record income you have not told them about.
  • Check whether the accountant's fee includes the software, or whether you need to buy it separately.

If you would prefer to hand over the MTD work, an online accountancy service built for sole traders and small businesses is one option to consider.

Option 3: A mix of both

Many people fall somewhere in between. Because MTD allows one main agent and any number of supporting agents, you can split the work. Two common arrangements:

  • You keep the records, an accountant reviews them. You keep day-to-day records in software, and an accountant checks them or handles the parts you are less sure about.
  • You keep the records, a bookkeeper tidies them. You record transactions, and a bookkeeper tidies them up periodically.

This approach can keep you close to your numbers while giving you a safety net. Agree clearly who submits each update, so nothing is missed or duplicated.

Questions to help you decide

Work through these honestly:

  1. How many income sources do you have? One simple source favours software. Several sources, or a mix of trading and property income, favour professional help.
  2. How organised are your records now? Clean spreadsheets could pair well with bridging software. Scattered receipts suggest you may benefit from help.
  3. How much time can you give each quarter? Four updates a year is a regular commitment.
  4. How confident are you with tax categories? If you are unsure what counts as qualifying income or allowable expenses, an accountant can reduce the risk of errors.
  5. What would you do with the time saved? For some people, that time is worth more than the fee.

Working through the GOV.UK example

Take the GOV.UK example of £25,000 rental income and £27,000 self-employment income. That person has £52,000 of qualifying income and two different income sources to report.

If they choose software, it must support both self-employment and property income, as GOV.UK advises.

If they find keeping two sets of records demanding, an accountant acting as their main agent could manage both.

Neither choice is automatically right. It depends on their confidence, time and how organised their records are.

Groundwork that helps either way

Whatever you choose, keeping business money separate makes MTD easier.

Sole traders are not legally required to have a business bank account. However, separating personal and business finances:

  • makes business transactions easier to find
  • makes cash flow easier to track
  • simplifies your records for tax

When choosing an account:

  • Traditional banks can take longer to set up but may offer branches, overdrafts and loans.
  • App-based providers set up online quickly but may offer limited overdrafts.

Cleaner records save you time if you do it yourself. If you use an accountant, they save the accountant time too, which may affect what you pay.

Summary

  • Software on your own tends to suit people with simple affairs, organised records and the time to keep them up to date. Free options exist for simple cases.
  • An accountant tends to suit people with several income sources, complex arrangements, or little time or appetite for admin.
  • A mix of both suits people who want to stay involved but also want support.

Start by confirming your qualifying income and start date on GOV.UK. Then pick the approach that fits how you work. If you decide professional support is right for you, an online accountancy service is one route to explore.
