MTD Ready

Landlords with letting agents: getting ready for MTD

Who this guide is for

If a letting agent finds tenants, collects rent or manages repairs for you, Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax can still apply to you. The tax obligations sit with you as the landlord, not with the agent who manages your property. This guide covers whether MTD applies, how to work out your qualifying income, what a letting agent can and cannot do for you under MTD, and how to set up records so quarterly updates are manageable.

When MTD for Income Tax applies

MTD for Income Tax is being introduced in stages, based on your qualifying income in an earlier tax year:

  • From 6 April 2026, if your qualifying income was more than £50,000 in the 2024 to 2025 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2027, if your qualifying income is more than £30,000 in the 2025 to 2026 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2028, if your qualifying income is more than £20,000 in the 2026 to 2027 tax year.

If you fall within MTD, you must:

  1. keep digital records of your income and expenses
  2. use compatible software to send quarterly updates of income and expenses to HMRC
  3. sign up before your start date

Working out your qualifying income

Qualifying income is your total income from self-employment and property before expenses, which is your turnover rather than your profit.

This matters for landlords who use letting agents. Many agents deduct their fees before paying rent over to you, so the money that arrives in your bank account is lower than the rent your tenants paid. Letting agent fees are an expense, and qualifying income is measured before expenses. Look at the gross rent figure on your agent's statements, not just the net amount you received, when you check whether you are over a threshold. If you are unsure how a specific charge should be treated, check GOV.UK.

Some income does not count towards qualifying income:

  • employment (PAYE) income
  • your share of partnership profit
  • dividends
  • the State Pension
  • private pensions

For jointly owned property, only your share counts.

GOV.UK gives two examples:

  • Someone with £25,000 of rental income and £27,000 of self-employment income has £52,000 of qualifying income.
  • A jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000 of qualifying income.

So a landlord who also runs a small business can cross a threshold even if neither source would do so alone. Joint owners should each work out their own share.

Letting agents and tax agents are not the same thing

Under MTD, the word "agent" usually means a tax agent, such as an accountant or bookkeeper, who deals with HMRC on your behalf. A letting agent manages your property. Some firms offer both services, but many do not.

Under MTD for Income Tax:

  • You can have one main agent at a time. A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax.
  • You can have any number of supporting agents. A supporting agent has limited access and can only see business and property income details.
  • An agent, such as an accountant or bookkeeper, can sign you up for MTD with your permission.

If your letting agent says it can help with MTD, ask exactly what it will do. Does it act as a tax agent and send updates to HMRC for you, or does it only provide statements you or your accountant then use? For the rules on who can be authorised as a main or supporting agent, check GOV.UK.

If your letting agent only provides statements, the responsibility for keeping digital records and sending quarterly updates stays with you or your tax agent.

Getting usable information from your letting agent

Quarterly updates mean you need your property figures more often than once a year. Before your start date, it is worth agreeing with your letting agent:

  • How often you get statements. Monthly statements make it easier to keep records up to date through each quarter.
  • What the statements show. Ideally, each statement shows rent collected, each fee and charge, and any repair or maintenance costs paid on your behalf, listed separately rather than as one net figure.
  • The format. A downloadable file or a clear digital statement is easier to enter into software than a paper letter.
  • Property-by-property detail. If you own several properties, a breakdown by property helps you check figures and spot mistakes.

Remember to include income and costs the letting agent does not handle, such as expenses you pay yourself or a property you manage directly.

Quarterly update deadlines

You can use standard or calendar quarterly periods. The deadlines are the same either way.

Standard period Calendar period Deadline
6 April to 5 July 1 April to 30 June 7 August
6 April to 5 October 1 April to 30 September 7 November
6 April to 5 January 1 April to 31 December 7 February
6 April to 5 April 1 April to 31 March 7 May

Quarterly updates are cumulative. Each one covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of the update period. If a letting agent statement arrives late or you find an error, you can correct your records and the next update will reflect it, without resubmitting earlier quarters. Quarterly updates are summaries, not tax returns.

HMRC will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates for the 2026 to 2027 tax year. That gives you some room to settle into a routine, but aim to meet the deadlines from the start. For details of the year-end process and penalties in later years, check GOV.UK.

Keeping property money separate

Sole traders are not legally required to have a business bank account. Separating personal and business finances does make accounting simpler, though: business transactions are easier to find, cash flow is easier to track, and records for tax are simpler.

For landlords, having letting agent payments and property expenses go through one account means fewer personal transactions to sort through each quarter. When choosing an account, traditional banks can take longer to set up but may offer branches, overdrafts and loans. App-based providers can be set up online quickly but may offer limited overdrafts. For guidance on accounts for jointly owned property, check GOV.UK.

Choosing MTD software

GOV.UK suggests you:

  1. use its software finder to find compatible products
  2. choose between software that creates digital records and bridging software that works with spreadsheets
  3. check the software supports your income sources (for example, UK property, and self-employment if you have it) and your accounting period

Free options exist for people with simple tax affairs, so check those if you have one property and few transactions.

Bridging software may suit you if you already keep good spreadsheets from your letting agent statements. Full record-keeping software may suit you better if you have several properties, several income sources, or work with an accountant who uses the same system.

If you want software that creates digital records rather than bridging from a spreadsheet, Xero is one option to consider. Before committing, confirm on the GOV.UK software finder that it supports property income and your accounting period, and check that it suits how you and any accountant work.

Xero runs an affiliate programme that is free to join, so some websites that recommend it may earn commission. Judge any product on whether it meets your own needs and appears on the GOV.UK software finder.

A practical checklist

  • Check your qualifying income for the relevant tax year, using gross rent and only your share of any jointly owned property.
  • Find your start date from the thresholds above.
  • Talk to your letting agent about statement frequency, detail and format.
  • Decide who sends your updates: you, or an accountant or bookkeeper acting as your main agent.
  • Consider a separate account for property income and expenses.
  • Choose compatible software using the GOV.UK software finder.
  • Sign up before your start date, either yourself or through your agent with your permission.
  • Set reminders for 7 August, 7 November, 7 February and 7 May.

Summary

Using a letting agent does not take you outside MTD for Income Tax. Measure your qualifying income using gross rent, before expenses such as agent fees. Be clear about whether your letting agent is also acting as a tax agent. Make sure the information you get from your agent is detailed and frequent enough to support quarterly digital records. With those pieces in place, and software that suits your setup, the quarterly routine becomes much easier to manage.
