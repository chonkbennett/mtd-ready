Near the £30,000 MTD threshold? How to check carefully

The short answer

If you are a sole trader or landlord, the £30,000 threshold is about your qualifying income for the 2025 to 2026 tax year. If that figure is more than £30,000, Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax applies to you from 6 April 2027.

Qualifying income is your total income from self-employment and property before expenses, which means your turnover, not your profit. Many people near the line get the answer wrong because they look at profit or include income that does not count. This guide explains how to check your position step by step.

Which threshold applies, and when

GOV.UK sets out three stages:

  • From 6 April 2026: MTD for Income Tax applies if your qualifying income was more than £50,000 in the 2024 to 2025 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2027: it applies if your qualifying income is more than £30,000 in the 2025 to 2026 tax year.
  • From 6 April 2028: it applies if your qualifying income is more than £20,000 in the 2026 to 2027 tax year.

Each stage looks back at an earlier tax year. For the £30,000 stage, the year that matters is 2025 to 2026. The threshold is "more than £30,000", so the question is whether your qualifying income goes above that figure.

If your income is likely to fall under the £30,000 line but above £20,000, the 2028 stage may still be relevant, depending on your 2026 to 2027 income.

What counts as qualifying income

According to GOV.UK, qualifying income is the total of:

  • your self-employment income before expenses
  • your property income before expenses

The following do not count:

  • employment (PAYE) income
  • your share of partnership profit
  • dividends
  • the State Pension
  • private pensions

For jointly owned property, only your share of the income counts.

These rules cut both ways. Leaving out expenses can push your figure higher than you expected. Leaving out salary, pensions or dividends can bring it lower.

Step-by-step: checking your figure

  1. List every self-employment business you run. Add together the turnover (total sales or takings) from all of them for the tax year in question, before deducting any costs.
  2. List every property income source. Include the total rent and other property income before expenses. If you own a property jointly, include only your share.
  3. Leave out anything that does not count. Remove PAYE wages, partnership profit shares, dividends and pensions.
  4. Add the two totals together. This is your qualifying income.
  5. Compare it with the threshold for that year. For the 2027 start, compare your 2025 to 2026 figure with £30,000.

If you are unsure whether a particular type of income counts, for example an unusual receipt that is neither trading nor rental income, check the GOV.UK guidance on working out qualifying income rather than guessing.

Worked examples from GOV.UK

GOV.UK gives this example: someone with £25,000 of rental income and £27,000 of self-employment income has £52,000 of qualifying income. Neither source is over £50,000 on its own, but together they are. The sources are added.

For joint ownership, GOV.UK's example is a property earning £50,000 that is owned equally. Each owner has £25,000 of qualifying income from it, not £50,000.

Common near-threshold situations

Suppose you earn a salary from employment and also have self-employment turnover of £28,000 in 2025 to 2026, with no property income. Your salary does not count, so your qualifying income would be £28,000. That is not more than £30,000.

For example, someone might have £18,000 of self-employment turnover and £13,000 of rental income in 2025 to 2026. Their profit after expenses might be well under £30,000, but their qualifying income is £31,000 because expenses are not deducted. That is more than £30,000.

Suppose a couple jointly and equally own a rental property bringing in £40,000 a year, and one of them also has £12,000 of self-employment turnover. That person's qualifying income would be £20,000 (their half of the rent) plus £12,000, giving £32,000.

These are illustrations only. Your own figures and circumstances decide your position.

If you are close to the line

When your figure is near £30,000, accuracy matters more:

  • Use actual records, not estimates. Totals from invoices, sales records and rent statements are more reliable than memory.
  • Check you have not deducted expenses. It is easy to pick up a profit figure by mistake.
  • Confirm ownership shares on any jointly held property.
  • Keep a note of how you reached your figure in case you need to revisit it.

This guide does not cover what happens if your income moves above or below the threshold in later years, any exemptions, or how HMRC will contact you. If any of these apply, check GOV.UK.

Separating business and personal money can make this check easier. Sole traders are not legally required to have a business bank account. However, GOV.UK's business guidance says keeping business and personal finances separate makes business transactions easier to find, cash flow easier to track and tax records simpler. Traditional banks can take longer to set up but may offer branches, overdrafts and loans. App-based providers can be set up online quickly but may offer limited overdrafts.

What MTD for Income Tax involves if you are over

If you are within MTD for Income Tax, you must:

  • keep digital records
  • use compatible software to send quarterly updates of income and expenses to HMRC
  • sign up before your start date

Quarterly updates using standard periods cover 6 April to 5 July, 5 October, 5 January and 5 April. The deadlines are 7 August, 7 November, 7 February and 7 May. If you use calendar periods (1 April to 30 June, 30 September, 31 December and 31 March), the deadlines are the same.

The updates are cumulative. Each one covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of that update period. This means you can correct earlier records without resubmitting earlier quarters. Quarterly updates are summaries, not tax returns.

HMRC has said it will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates in the 2026 to 2027 tax year. If you join from April 2027, check GOV.UK for the rules that will apply to you.

Choosing software

GOV.UK suggests you:

  • use its software finder
  • decide between software that creates digital records and bridging software that works with spreadsheets
  • check the software supports your income sources and accounting period

GOV.UK notes that free options exist for people with simple tax affairs, so paid software is not essential for everyone.

If you would rather keep your records in dedicated accounting software than in spreadsheets, Xero is one option to consider. Before choosing it or any other product, check that it appears in the GOV.UK software finder and supports your mix of self-employment and property income and your accounting period.

Getting help from an agent

You do not have to handle MTD alone. Under MTD for Income Tax, you can have one main agent at a time and any number of supporting agents:

  • A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax.
  • A supporting agent has limited access and can see only business and property income details.

An accountant or bookkeeper can sign you up with your permission. If your qualifying income is borderline or complicated, an agent can help you confirm the figure.

Summary

  • The £30,000 threshold applies to your 2025 to 2026 qualifying income, with MTD starting 6 April 2027 if you are over it.
  • Qualifying income is self-employment plus property turnover, before expenses.
  • PAYE income, partnership profit shares, dividends and pensions do not count. For joint property, count only your share.
  • If you are close to the line, work from actual records and check the GOV.UK guidance on any point not covered here.
