MTD Ready

Why a separate business account makes MTD records easier

Do you need a business bank account for MTD?

No. Sole traders and ordinary partnerships are not legally required to have a business bank account, and opening one is not part of signing up for Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax.

What MTD does require is that, once you are within it, you:

  • keep digital records of your income and expenses
  • use compatible software to send quarterly updates of income and expenses to HMRC
  • sign up before your start date

A separate account is a practical choice, not a legal one. GOV.UK notes that separating personal and business finances makes accounting simpler. Business transactions are easier to find, cash flow is easier to track and records for tax are simpler. Those three benefits matter more under MTD because you report to HMRC every quarter rather than once a year.

Who MTD for Income Tax applies to, and when

MTD for Income Tax is being phased in according to your qualifying income:

Start date Required if qualifying income was more than… In the tax year
6 April 2026 £50,000 2024 to 2025
6 April 2027 £30,000 2025 to 2026
6 April 2028 £20,000 2026 to 2027

Qualifying income is your total income from self-employment and property before expenses, which means your turnover rather than your profit. The following do not count:

  • employment (PAYE) income
  • your share of partnership profit
  • dividends
  • the State Pension and private pensions

For jointly owned property, only your share counts.

GOV.UK gives two examples:

  • £25,000 of rental income plus £27,000 of self-employment income gives £52,000 of qualifying income.
  • A jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000.

Because qualifying income is measured before expenses, you need a clear figure for your business and property turnover. That is far easier to produce when those receipts land in one place, rather than mixed in with your salary, refunds and transfers from family.

Why a mixed account creates extra work under MTD

When personal and business money share one account, someone has to sort every transaction. Each one is either business income, a business expense, or personal and to be ignored. Once a year this is tedious. Under MTD you face that sorting four times a year.

Quarterly updates are cumulative. Each one covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of the update period. That design has an advantage: if you spot a miscategorised transaction later, you can correct earlier records without resubmitting earlier quarters. Quarterly updates are also summaries, not tax returns.

Even so, every quarter you still need your records to be accurate enough to summarise. A mixed account makes that harder in a few ways:

  • More transactions to review. Your groceries, subscriptions and personal transfers all appear alongside business items. Each one has to be checked and excluded.
  • Higher risk of errors. It is easy to miss an income payment among personal credits, or to include a personal purchase as an expense.
  • Harder to explain later. If you or an agent need to trace a figure, a mixed statement takes longer to read.

What a separate account changes in practice

With a dedicated account for your business or property activity, most of the sorting happens automatically:

  1. Business transactions are easier to find. Nearly everything in the account relates to your business, so the job shifts from "which of these are business?" to "which category does each business item belong in?"
  2. Cash flow is easier to track. You can see what has come in and gone out of the business without personal spending blurring the picture. That helps you plan for tax and quieter months.
  3. Records for tax are simpler. Your digital records, whether in software or a spreadsheet, can follow the account more closely. That gives your quarterly summaries a cleaner starting point.

For example, a sole trader whose personal current account shows a few hundred transactions in a quarter might find that only a fraction relate to the business. With a separate account, those few are the only ones to categorise.

A separate account will not remove every judgement call. Some costs may be partly business and partly personal. The rules on what you can claim are not covered here, so check GOV.UK for current guidance on allowable expenses.

Landlords: property income and joint ownership

Landlords benefit in the same way. A separate account for rent received and property costs makes your property turnover easier to see. That figure feeds directly into whether you meet the qualifying income threshold.

If you own property jointly, remember that only your share counts towards qualifying income. In the GOV.UK example, a property earning £50,000 split equally gives each owner £25,000. If your arrangement is more complicated, such as unequal shares or several properties with different co-owners, check GOV.UK for how to work out and record your share.

Matching your account to your software

GOV.UK advises using its software finder and choosing between two broad types of software:

  • software that creates digital records
  • bridging software that works with spreadsheets

It also says to check that the software supports your income sources (self-employment, property, or both) and your accounting period. Free options exist for people with simple tax affairs.

Before you open an account, it is worth checking whether the software you are considering can connect to it or import its transactions. Confirm this with the software provider and the bank directly, as compatibility can change. A separate account combined with software that can read it is what makes quarterly record-keeping quicker.

Choosing a business account

GOV.UK describes two broad types of provider:

  • Traditional banks can take longer to set up but may offer branches, overdrafts and loans.
  • App-based providers set up online quickly but may offer limited overdrafts.

Decide which matters more to you: in-person support and borrowing options, or a quick online set-up. Then compare fees, eligibility and how well each account works with your chosen MTD software. Check each provider's current terms on its own website.

Getting set up before your quarters begin

It is simpler to start a separate account at the beginning of a tax year or quarter, so each update period draws on one clean source. The quarterly periods and deadlines are:

Standard period Calendar period Deadline
6 April – 5 July 1 April – 30 June 7 August
6 July – 5 October 1 July – 30 September 7 November
6 October – 5 January 1 October – 31 December 7 February
6 January – 5 April 1 January – 31 March 7 May

HMRC will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates for the 2026 to 2027 tax year. That gives first-year users some room to settle into a routine. It is still sensible to build good habits from the start. For anything beyond that first year, or for other penalties, check GOV.UK.

A practical sequence:

  1. Work out your qualifying income to see when MTD applies to you.
  2. Choose compatible software using the GOV.UK software finder.
  3. Open a separate account and confirm it works with that software.
  4. Route all business or property income and expenses through it.
  5. Sign up for MTD for Income Tax before your start date.

Working with an agent

Under MTD for Income Tax you can have one main agent at a time and any number of supporting agents. A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax. A supporting agent has limited access and can only see business and property income details. An agent, such as an accountant or bookkeeper, can sign you up with your permission.

A separate account helps here too. Your agent spends less time separating personal items from business ones, which can make their work on your records more straightforward.

Summary

A business bank account is not legally required for MTD for Income Tax, but it makes keeping digital records and sending cumulative quarterly updates simpler. Business transactions are easier to find, cash flow is easier to track and your tax records are cleaner. Work out your qualifying income, choose compatible software, then choose an account that suits how you work.
