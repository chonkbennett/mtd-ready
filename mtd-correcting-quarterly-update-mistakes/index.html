Made a mistake in an MTD quarterly update? How to fix it

The short answer

If you've sent a Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax quarterly update and then found a mistake, such as a missed invoice, an expense in the wrong category or a figure keyed in twice, you usually don't need to resubmit the earlier quarter.

GOV.UK explains that quarterly updates are cumulative. Each update covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of that update period. So you can correct the error in your digital records, and the corrected figures will flow into your next quarterly update. GOV.UK states that this means earlier records can be corrected without resubmitting earlier quarters.

GOV.UK also says that quarterly updates are summaries, not tax returns. A mistake in one is not the same as an error on a filed tax return.

The rest of this guide explains how this works, the common mistakes to look for, and how to reduce the chance of making them.

Why cumulative updates make corrections simpler

Under MTD for Income Tax, you keep digital records and use compatible software to send quarterly updates of your income and expenses to HMRC. If you use the standard update periods, they run from 6 April to:

  • 5 July (deadline 7 August)
  • 5 October (deadline 7 November)
  • 5 January (deadline 7 February)
  • 5 April (deadline 7 May)

If you have chosen calendar periods, your updates run from 1 April to 30 June, 30 September, 31 December and 31 March. The deadlines are the same as for standard periods.

Each update runs from the start of the tax year, not just from the end of the previous quarter. So your second update includes everything in the first quarter as well as the second. If you correct a first-quarter figure in your records before sending the second update, the second update will show the corrected year-to-date totals.

Step by step: fixing a mistake

1. Find the error in your records. Work out exactly what went wrong. It might be a missing transaction, a duplicate, a wrong amount, the wrong category, or income recorded against the wrong business or property.

2. Correct the digital record itself. Fix the underlying transaction in your software or spreadsheet. Don't add a balancing figure. Your digital records should reflect what actually happened.

3. Check the year-to-date totals. Before your next update, review the cumulative figures your software is about to send. Make sure the correction appears in the right income source and category.

4. Send your next quarterly update as normal. Because it covers the period from the start of the tax year, it will include the corrected figures.

5. Keep a note of what you changed. A brief note of the date, the original error and the fix will help if you or an agent need to review the year later.

If you're unsure whether a particular error needs any other action, for example if it's large or you've found it after your final update for the year, check GOV.UK or ask your agent.

A worked illustration

Suppose a sole trader records a sales invoice twice in their first quarter, so their first update overstates income. In the second quarter, they spot the duplicate and delete it from their records. When they send their second update, the year-to-date income figure is correct, because it's recalculated from the corrected records for the whole period since 6 April. They don't send a new version of the first update.

This is an illustration of how cumulative updates work, not a rule about any specific amount.

Common mistakes to check for

Putting income under the wrong source

If you have both self-employment and property income, make sure each transaction sits under the right one. GOV.UK says to check that your software supports all your income sources. If it doesn't, you may find it harder to keep them separate.

Recording the whole of a jointly owned property

For jointly owned property, only your share counts as your qualifying income. GOV.UK gives this example: a jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000. If you've recorded the full rental income as yours, correct your records to show your share. If you're unsure how the split should apply to your records, check GOV.UK.

Mixing personal and business transactions

A personal purchase recorded as a business expense, or the other way round, is a common error. Sole traders aren't legally required to have a business bank account. However, GOV.UK's business guidance notes that keeping personal and business finances separate makes accounting simpler. Business transactions are easier to find, cash flow is easier to track and tax records are simpler.

Using the wrong update period

If your records are set up for standard periods but you meant to use calendar periods (or the reverse), transactions near the end of a quarter may fall into the wrong update. Check which periods you've chosen, and make sure your software matches.

Misjudging whether you're in MTD at all

Sometimes the "mistake" is about eligibility rather than an update. Qualifying income is your total income from self-employment and property before expenses. Employment (PAYE) income, your share of partnership profit, dividends, the State Pension and private pensions don't count.

GOV.UK's example: £25,000 of rental income plus £27,000 of self-employment income gives £52,000 of qualifying income.

The thresholds are:

  • From 6 April 2026: qualifying income over £50,000 in the 2024 to 2025 tax year
  • From 6 April 2027: qualifying income over £30,000 in the 2025 to 2026 tax year
  • From 6 April 2028: qualifying income over £20,000 in the 2026 to 2027 tax year

If you're in scope, you must sign up before your start date.

What if the mistake was sending an update late?

GOV.UK states that HMRC will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates for the 2026 to 2027 tax year. For later tax years, check GOV.UK for the penalty rules that apply. Either way, it's sensible to send any overdue update as soon as you can, so that your records and HMRC's information stay up to date.

What about mistakes found at the end of the year?

The cumulative approach covers corrections between quarterly updates. If you find an error after your final quarterly update for the tax year, or after you've completed your year-end tax return, the process may be different. These facts aren't covered here, so check GOV.UK for the current guidance before you act.

Reducing mistakes in future

  • Keep your digital records up to date. Recording transactions as they happen makes errors easier to spot before an update is due.
  • Choose software that fits your situation. GOV.UK recommends using its software finder. It suggests choosing between software that creates digital records and bridging software that works with spreadsheets, and checking that the software supports your income sources and accounting period. Free options exist for simple tax affairs.
  • Review before you submit. Compare your year-to-date totals with your bank statements before each update.
  • Consider getting help from an agent. Under MTD for Income Tax, you can have one main agent at a time and any number of supporting agents. A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax. A supporting agent has limited access and can only see business and property income details. An agent, such as an accountant or bookkeeper, can also sign you up with your permission.

If you'd prefer software that creates digital records directly, rather than bridging from a spreadsheet, Xero is one option to consider. Before choosing it, confirm on the GOV.UK software finder that it supports your income sources and accounting period.

Summary

A mistake in an MTD quarterly update is usually straightforward to fix. Because updates are cumulative and are summaries rather than tax returns, you can correct the underlying digital record and the next update will carry the corrected year-to-date figures. You don't need to resubmit earlier quarters. For errors found after the year's final update, or for penalty rules beyond the 2026 to 2027 tax year, check GOV.UK for the latest guidance.
