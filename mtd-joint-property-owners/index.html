MTD for jointly owned property: how your share is counted

The short answer

If you own a rental property with someone else, you do not count the whole property's rental income when deciding whether Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax applies to you. GOV.UK says that for jointly owned property, only your share counts towards your qualifying income.

That one rule can change your start date, or whether you need to join MTD at all yet. The rest of this guide explains how qualifying income is worked out, how your share fits in, and what to do next.

What counts as qualifying income

MTD for Income Tax is triggered by your qualifying income. GOV.UK defines this as your total income from self-employment and property before expenses, which means turnover, not profit.

These do not count towards qualifying income:

  • Employment (PAYE) income
  • A partner's share of partnership profit
  • Dividends
  • The State Pension
  • Private pensions

Because qualifying income is measured before expenses, a property with high costs such as repairs, letting agent fees or mortgage interest still counts at its full rental income figure for this test. The calculation uses your share of the rent received, not your share of the profit.

How your share of a jointly owned property is counted

GOV.UK gives this example: a jointly owned property earning £50,000, owned equally, gives each owner £25,000 of qualifying income.

So each co-owner tests their own £25,000 against the MTD thresholds, not the full £50,000. A common mistake is to look at the total rent on the tenancy agreement or the letting agent's statement and assume that figure is yours. For MTD purposes, it is your share that matters.

What if ownership is not equal?

The verified GOV.UK guidance confirms that only your share counts, but the facts in this guide do not cover how your share is determined when ownership is unequal, or the special rules that may apply to married couples and civil partners. If your property is not owned 50:50, or you have changed how income is split, check the qualifying income guidance on GOV.UK before relying on a figure.

Combining your share with other income

Your share of a jointly owned property is added to any other self-employment and property income you have. This is where joint owners can cross a threshold they did not expect.

GOV.UK's example shows that £25,000 of rental income plus £27,000 of self-employment income makes £52,000 of qualifying income.

Put that together with the jointly owned property example. If your £25,000 is your equal share of a £50,000 property, and you also earn £27,000 from self-employment, your qualifying income is £52,000. That is more than £50,000. Your co-owner, if they have no other qualifying income, has £25,000. Two people sharing the same property can end up with different MTD start dates.

Remember that a salary, pension income or dividends would not be added in. Only self-employment and property turnover count.

Which start date applies to you

GOV.UK sets out these thresholds. Each is based on qualifying income in a specific earlier tax year:

MTD required from If qualifying income was more than In the tax year
6 April 2026 £50,000 2024 to 2025
6 April 2027 £30,000 2025 to 2026
6 April 2028 £20,000 2026 to 2027

Using the GOV.UK figures, a £25,000 share on its own is not more than £50,000 or £30,000, but it is more than £20,000. So for that tax year's income it would matter for the 2028 threshold. Always test the correct tax year's figures against each threshold.

What MTD means once you are in

People within MTD for Income Tax must:

  • keep digital records of income and expenses
  • use compatible software to send quarterly updates to HMRC
  • sign up before their start date

Quarterly update periods and deadlines

Standard period Calendar period Deadline
6 April to 5 July 1 April to 30 June 7 August
6 April to 5 October 1 April to 30 September 7 November
6 April to 5 January 1 April to 31 December 7 February
6 April to 5 April 1 April to 31 March 7 May

Quarterly updates are cumulative. Each one covers the period from the start of the tax year to the end of the update period. If you find an error in an earlier quarter, such as your co-owner's agent sending a late statement, you can correct it in a later update without resubmitting earlier quarters. Quarterly updates are summaries, not tax returns.

HMRC has said it will not give penalty points for late quarterly updates for the 2026 to 2027 tax year. This gives you some room to adjust, but it is still sensible to build a routine early.

The facts here do not set out exactly how a jointly owned property should be entered in quarterly updates. Check the "send quarterly updates" guidance on GOV.UK.

Keeping joint property records tidy

Joint ownership adds a step: you need to see your share clearly, separate from your co-owner's and from your personal spending.

GOV.UK's business guidance notes that sole traders are not legally required to have a business bank account. However, separating personal and business finances makes accounting simpler. Transactions are easier to find, cash flow is easier to track, and tax records are simpler. Traditional banks can take longer to set up but may offer branches, overdrafts and loans. App-based providers can be set up online quickly but may offer limited overdrafts.

For a jointly owned property, practical steps include:

  • agreeing with your co-owner how rent and costs will be recorded
  • keeping copies of letting agent statements showing the full property figures
  • recording your share consistently every quarter

Choosing software

GOV.UK recommends that you:

  1. use its software finder to see compatible products
  2. choose between software that creates digital records and bridging software that connects spreadsheets to HMRC
  3. check the software supports your income sources and accounting period, so property income and any self-employment income are both covered

Free options exist for people with simple tax affairs, so you do not have to pay for software to comply.

If you want a single place to track property income alongside self-employment, accounting software such as Xero is one option to compare. Check it in the GOV.UK software finder and confirm it supports your income types before you decide.

Working with an agent

Under MTD for Income Tax you can have one main agent and any number of supporting agents. A main agent can fully manage your MTD for Income Tax. A supporting agent has limited access and can see only business and property income details. An agent such as an accountant or bookkeeper can sign you up, with your permission.

This can help joint owners who use different accountants, or who have a bookkeeper for the property and an accountant overall.

Quick checklist

  • Work out your share of each jointly owned property's rental income, before expenses
  • Add any other property and self-employment turnover, but not PAYE, pensions, dividends or partnership profit share
  • Compare the total against the right threshold and tax year
  • If ownership is unequal or you are married or in a civil partnership, check GOV.UK for how your share is set
  • Pick compatible software using the GOV.UK finder
  • Sign up before your start date, yourself or through an agent
